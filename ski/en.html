🔁 First principles: one closed loop, nobody in charge

Simplification: the whole body is treated as riding the board’s line. The centripetal force a turn needs can’t come from your legs — only from the snow: F·Δt = Δp. You only give inputs (tilt, pressure, twist); the sidecut sets the arc, the snow’s push changes your momentum, the body aligns to bear that force, and that body position becomes the next input. In 3D: cyan = momentum p、green = Δp (¼ s)、orange = the snow force physics requires、purple = body alignment; orange on purple = balanced.

⚖️ Where is your balance?

Yellow ball = centre of mass (CoM), roughly behind the navel in the middle of the pelvis. The line drops from the CoM to the snow along the body’s lean (the direction the snow force should pass through), not straight down; the circle is where it lands (green = balanced, orange = drifting, red = off). So when the body leans into a turn, the circle should still be near the feet. Gliding straight, fore–aft it should be mid-foot; entering and leaving a turn the CoM moves along the arc — it’s not a fixed spot.

How to find your own balance point (at home / on snow)
  1. Stand in your boots, knees and ankles slightly bent, and rock slowly forward and back: at the heels or toes you feel about to fall; the steadiest point in between is right (mid-foot, shins lightly on the tongues).
  2. Close your eyes and rock again, feeling the pressure move from heel to toe.
  3. Hop lightly on the spot: if you land without grabbing anything or sitting back, your balance is right.
  4. While skiing keep your hands in front where you can see them; hands drifting behind usually means you are sitting back.
  5. On flat terrain, lift one ski’s tail while the tip stays on the snow: if you are sitting back you can’t.

Why is the inside leg working too? ① The inside ski still carries about 10–40% of body weight (more on hard snow and at high edge angles); ② to shorten the inside leg the quads have to lengthen under control, not switch off; ③ the hamstrings and hip flexors pull the inside foot back and up to keep both feet level fore/aft; ④ the tibialis anterior keeps the inside boot tongue engaged so the inside ski doesn’t sit back; ⑤ muscles around the joints co-contract to keep the knee stable and catch you if the outside ski slips. So the outside leg does the main work, and the inside leg handles ’fine-tuning and insurance’.

Contraction types are estimated from the model’s joint-angle changes, not measured by EMG: a muscle estimated to lengthen while working = eccentric, shorten = concentric, barely change = isometric. Real effort changes with speed, slope and snow, and muscles around a joint also co-contract.

Ski R = sidecut radius (the R number printed on the ski). When carving, turn radius ≈ R × cos(edge angle) and lean angle comes from tan θ = v² / (g·r), so speed, R and edge are all linked. This is an idealised formula: real skis bend deeper and the snow packs down, so you can actually carve a bit faster. Recommended ranges are rules of thumb, not hard limits.
